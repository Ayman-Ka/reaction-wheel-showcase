01 How it works

How the reaction wheel balances the frame

When the wheel speeds up, it pushes the frame the opposite way. A frame standing on its edge falls over by itself, so the firmware corrects it 1,000 times a second. Satellites use reaction wheels the same way to point themselves, and this is a desk-sized version of that.

  1. Sense

    Measure

    An encoder reads the wheel's angle over SPI 10,000 times a second, and an IMU reads the frame's gravity vector and rotation over I²C 1,000 times a second.

  2. Decide

    Compute

    A four-state LQR controller takes the tilt, tilt rate, wheel speed and how far the wheel has turned, and calculates one motor voltage from them.

  3. Act

    Drive

    The FOC driver converts that voltage into three sine-wave PWM signals for the motor driver board, so the wheel gets smooth torque in both directions.

04 Wiring

Breadboard layout and pin map

This is a breadboard bench prototype. The 12 V from the PSU and the three motor phases connect straight to the driver board, and everything else runs off the USB-C port with 3.3 V logic and one shared ground.

Design choices

Two I²C buses

I put the IMU and the display on separate buses, because redrawing one line of text on the display takes about 6 ms of bus time and would block the 1 kHz IMU reads if they shared one.

EN as the kill switch

Pulling EN low turns off every driver output and lets the wheel coast. The DRV8313 already pulls EN low internally, and the firmware drives it low as the first thing at startup, so the motor can't move until the firmware enables it.

Wiring the fault lines

On over-current the DRV8313 latches off until its reset pin is pulsed or the power is cycled, so I wired both lines to the STM32. The firmware watches the fault pin and can clear the fault without cutting power.

One timer for all three phases

IN1–IN3 run off TIM2 channels 1–3, so all three phases share one counter and one update event.

05 Motor control

Writing the FOC motor driver from scratch

A brushless motor makes the most torque when its magnetic field leads the rotor by 90 electrical degrees. I wrote the driver without a motor library, and every 100 µs the firmware reads the rotor angle, calculates where the field should point and writes the three PWM duties directly to the timer registers.

To get the electrical angle, the firmware multiplies the encoder angle by the 7 pole pairs and subtracts an offset that was measured once with an alignment sweep. An inverse Park transform rotates the requested voltage onto that angle, and an inverse Clarke transform splits it into three phase voltages 120° apart. Those become centred sine-wave duties on a 20 kHz carrier.

Voltage mode

The driver board doesn't have current sensors, so the firmware controls voltage instead, capped at 6 V, which is the full sine range on a 12 V supply. At 6 V, back-EMF limits the wheel to about 1,400 RPM.

Align once, store it

An automatic 9-second routine that runs once at setup locks the stator field at electrical zero and sweeps it one electrical revolution forward and back. The rotor follows it, turning the wheel about 51° and back, and the settled encoder reading gives the motor's direction and electrical offset, which I saved in the firmware. Since the encoder is absolute and its magnet is fixed to the rotor, the offset stays the same through power cycles, so startup never has to move the wheel. If the magnet is moved or the phase wires are swapped, setup has to be run again.

Updating all three duties together

The firmware holds off timer updates while it writes the three duties, so all three change on the same PWM cycle.
